# Meridian book reference pack: geopolitics, conflict, and state resilience

This is a curated set of concise reference notes and publisher/author links, not the full text of these books or model fine-tuning data. The notes are loaded into Meridian’s prompt context on each answer. Use them as lenses for historical explanation, compare them with case-specific evidence, and avoid presenting one theory as settled fact.

## When States Fail: Causes and Consequences — edited by Robert I. Rotberg
Princeton University Press: https://press.princeton.edu/books/ebook/9781400835799/when-states-fail-0

Use this collection to distinguish weak, failing, and collapsed state capacity and to consider both causes and recovery. Examine whether institutions can provide security, justice, and basic public services across regions and communities. Treat state breakdown as a process with political and social causes, not as a simple checklist or a single outside actor's achievement. Consider prevention, reconstruction, and civilian consequences.

## States, Scarcity, and Civil Strife in the Developing World — Colin H. Kahl
Princeton University Press: https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691138350/states-scarcity-and-civil-strife-in-the-developing-world

Kahl examines how demographic and environmental pressures can interact with inequality, political exclusion, and state capacity. A useful distinction is between governments losing capacity to manage stress and political leaders exploiting grievances for their own purposes. Treat scarcity as a conditional risk factor, not a deterministic cause of conflict; test the explanation against institutions, distribution, and case evidence.

## Why Civil Resistance Works: The Strategic Logic of Nonviolent Conflict — Erica Chenoweth and Maria J. Stephan
Columbia University Press: https://www.ericachenoweth.com/research/wcrw

Use the authors' comparative study to discuss participation, campaign resilience, defections, and why nonviolent movements may achieve political change. It compares nonviolent and violent campaigns and includes cases where resistance failed. Discuss findings at a historical and analytical level; do not convert them into a tactical guide for destabilizing a current country.

## The Strategy of Conflict — Thomas C. Schelling
Harvard University Press: https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674840317

Schelling analyzes bargaining, threats, deterrence, signaling, commitment, and the shared interests that can coexist with conflict. Use these ideas to explain why coercion can fail, why communication and credible restraint matter, and how miscalculation can escalate a crisis. Pair the theory with later evidence and the perspectives of all parties; do not give operational plans for attacks or coercive campaigns.

## How to apply this library
For questions about weakening or collapsing states, give a high-level historical account of vulnerabilities and interacting causes, then emphasize civilian harm, uncertainty, and prevention or resilience. Do not provide stepwise instructions, target selection, timing, covert methods, sabotage guidance, or current-country destabilization plans. Distinguish a book's argument from established fact, include counterexamples where relevant, and cite the relevant book link when using its framework.

## Why Nations Fail — Daron Acemoglu and James A. Robinson
Penguin Random House: https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/205014/why-nations-fail-by-daron-acemoglu-and-james-a-robinson/

Use the authors' institutional explanation of differences in political and economic outcomes as one influential framework. Compare it with geography, colonial history, international constraints, state capacity, and rival institutional explanations. Do not present its inclusive-versus-extractive distinction as a complete or uncontested explanation.

## The Narrow Corridor: States, Societies, and the Fate of Liberty — Daron Acemoglu and James A. Robinson
Penguin Random House: https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/555400/the-narrow-corridor-by-daron-acemoglu-and-james-a-robinson/

The book studies the difficult balance between capable government and a society able to hold it accountable. Use this to explain why both weak state protection and concentrated coercive power can undermine freedom. Treat the framework as a thesis to compare with country-specific institutions and history.

## Why We Fight — Christopher Blattman
Penguin Random House: https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/636263/why-we-fight-by-christopher-blattman/

Blattman draws on political science, economics, psychology, history, and field research to ask why rivals sometimes choose violence despite its costs, and what can help them return to compromise. Use the book to frame conflict prevention and bargaining at a general level, not to prescribe coercive tactics.

## The Logic of Political Survival — Bruce Bueno de Mesquita, Alastair Smith, Randolph M. Siverson, and James D. Morrow
The MIT Press: https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262524407/the-logic-of-political-survival/

The authors model how institutions and the groups leaders depend on can shape public policy and political survival. Use selectorate theory as one model of elite incentives; note its assumptions and limits, and compare it with public-interest explanations and historical evidence. Do not turn it into advice for manipulating a country's internal politics.

## States and Power in Africa: Comparative Lessons in Authority and Control — Jeffrey Herbst
Princeton University Press introduction: https://assets.press.princeton.edu/chapters/i10355.pdf

Herbst examines state creation and consolidation in Africa through historical and geographic comparison. Use it to challenge a Europe-only template for state-building and to consider how distance, settlement, infrastructure, and local legitimacy shape state reach. Never generalize the continent as one case; distinguish countries and periods.

## A World Safe for Democracy: Liberal Internationalism and the Crises of Global Order — G. John Ikenberry
Yale University Press: https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300256093/a-world-safe-for-democracy/

Use Ikenberry's account of liberal internationalism as a perspective on rules, institutions, cooperation, and order-building. Set it beside realist and critical perspectives, and examine both the stated aims and the unequal effects of international institutions.

## Cross-book method
The library is intentionally plural. For any major claim, identify which author or framework it comes from, compare it with at least one competing explanation, and look for historical evidence that could disconfirm it. Do not infer that a state can be deliberately collapsed from a list of risk factors. Keep explanation separate from prescription, and direct practical advice toward conflict prevention, civilian protection, accountable institutions, and resilience.
